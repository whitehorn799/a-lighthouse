# All-Or-Nothing

## All-or-Nothing Thinking:
One mistake makes a person a total failure.
## Overgeneralization:
Seeing a single negative event as a never-ending pattern of defeat.
## Magical Thinking: 
Believing your thoughts or actions can directly influence external events or expecting things to magically fix themselves.

# Jump-to-Conclusions
## Catastrophizing:
Assuming the worst outcome.

## Fortune Telling:
Predicting a negative future outcome as established fact before it happens.
## Mind Reading:
Assuming that you know exactly what someone else is thinking or feeling without real proof.

# Reality-Filtering Biases
## Negative Mental Filter: 
Focusing exclusively on a single negative detail and dwelling on it so completely that your perception of reality is darkened.
## Disqualifying the Positive:
Actively rejecting positive experiences by insisting they "don't count" for some reason and maintaining a negative belief even when faced with contradictory evidence.
## Emotional Reasoning:
Assuming that your negative emotions reflect objective truth ("I feel like an idiot, therefore I must be one").

# Rule-Making and Blaming
## Should Statements:
Trying to motivate yourself or judge others with rigid "shoulds," "musts," and "ought tos"; leading to guilt when applied to yourself and resentment to others.
## Personalization:
Holding yourself entirely responsible for an external event or another person's emotional state over which you had little or no control.
## Blaming:
Opposite of personalization; completely overlooking your own role in a problem and focusing entirely on how others are to blame for your misery.

# Judgmental 
## Labeling:
Attaching a rigid, global label to yourself or someone else instead of describing an error ("I'm a loser" instead of "I made a mistake").
## Emotional Reasoning/Validation Seeking: 
Assuming that because a situation is difficult or uncomfortable, you are being targeted, treated unfairly, or that the task is impossible.
## Fallacy of Change:
Expecting other people to change their behavior to suit your needs or happiness and believing that your emotional survival depends entirely on their transformation.

# The Control and Fairness Biases
## The Fallacy of Fairness:
Measuring every life event against a rigid, personal standard of "fairness." Because the universe does not operate on this standard, the person lives in a constant state of resentment, anger, and victimization.
## Control Fallacies (External):
Feeling completely helpless and viewing yourself as a passive victim of fate, luck, or other people's whims. (e.g., *"There is no point in trying to improve my health because my genetics have already ruined it."*)
## Control Fallacies (Internal):
Feeling completely responsible for the happiness, pain, and outcomes of everyone around you. This distortion drives people-pleasing and severe burnout.

## Omnipotence Fallacy:
The subconscious belief that you are the primary cause of everything that happens around you. If a meeting goes poorly or a friend is in a bad mood, you automatically assume it is because of something you did or failed to do.

# The Comparison and Worth Biases
## Social Comparison Bias (Upward Hyperfixation):
Constantly comparing your "behind-the-scenes" struggles against everyone else's "highlight reels." This creates a permanent filter of inadequacy and fuels *Main Character Syndrome* or bitterness.

## The Heaven's Reward Fallacy:
Expecting all your sacrifices, self-denial, and hard work to automatically pay off with a massive, magical reward later. When the reward doesn't arrive, it triggers deep bitterness, depression, and a "martyr complex."
## Discounting the Future (Present Bias):
Treating short-term emotional comfort as incredibly valuable while treating long-term consequences as irrelevant. This directly drives the behavior of staying in toxic loops or repeating downward spirals because escaping them requires short-term discomfort.

# The Cognitive Efficiency Short-Cuts
## Availability Heuristic Bias: 
Assuming that if something can be easily recalled or imagined, it is highly likely to happen. For example, if you can vividly picture a presentation going horribly wrong, your brain convinces you that disaster is inevitable.
## Anchoring Bias:
Tunnel-visioning on the very first piece of negative information you receive about a situation or a person, and refusing to update your opinion even when overwhelming positive data arrives later.
## Halo/Horns Effect (Splitting):
An extension of black-and-white thinking where you view a person as entirely good (Halo) or entirely bad (Horns). In a trauma bond, the victim applies the "Halo" to the abuser based on past love-bombing, completely ignoring the current abuse.

# The Defense and Avoidance Filters

## Minimization of the Self:
The exact opposite of catastrophizing. When you achieve something incredible, you minimize it to insignificance. (e.g., _"Anyone could have passed that exam, it wasn't a big deal"_), effectively starving your brain of well-deserved confidence.
## The "Always Right" Distortion:
Treating your own opinions and beliefs as absolute, unyielding facts. To this person, being wrong is psychologically unacceptable, so they will destroy relationships, gaslight others, and distort evidence just to defend their correctness.
## Pathologizing Normal Emotions: 
Concluding that experiencing completely normal human emotions—like grief, temporary sadness, anger, or nervousness—means you are fundamentally broken, mentally ill, or failing at your recovery.

## Temporal Discarding (Past-Present Disconnect):
Believing that because you failed or were mistreated in the past, your current reality and future are mathematically locked into that exact same outcome.
## Self-Fulfilling Prophecy Distortion:
Prematurely deciding that an event will go poorly, which changes your body language and behavior to be defensive or hostile, which then _causes_ the event to go poorly—validating your original negative thought.
