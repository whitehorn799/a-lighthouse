# 1. Why is negativity so noticeable?
The brain focuses so quickly on negativity because it's a risk management exercise, which is a plan to reduce risks and increase survivability. 

We are always in survival mode, and negativity directly impacts our survival. Our brain is trained to fixate on negativity because we are thinkers, problem solvers, and if we can't fix problems, then they become even more of a problem. Negatives are seen as potential risks, and the best survivors didn't prioritize enjoying rewards.
## 1.1 Negativity Bias
Have you ever been enjoying hours of peace and quiet, and then the moment an annoying sound appears, your total experience is ruined? You are instantly driven to stop the noise. What about receiving hundreds of compliments on your work, but the one or two negative comments creep into your mind at night? 

Our ancestors were survivors, and they had a bias toward negativity, which means they favored it more than positivity. It's not because they liked it more, but it's because they engaged in risk management in order to survive. People survived longer when they paid immediate attention to large animals that wanted to eat them.

That's the danger we see in negativity, but nowadays it's less about physical survival and more about social survival. Negativity is mentally heavy. It becomes a worry that is constantly on our mind. If we don't turn it into a positive, we're afraid that it will become much worse later. Problems that are ignored get worse.

Negativity can be easy to see, like when a tiger walks into your bedroom. It can be hard to see, like when someone gives a compliment but you interpret it as an insult. 


# 2. Why are people stuck in negativity?
We have to look into this question by dividing it: negative loop, a downward spiral, and misery.

## 2.1 Negative Loop
These are generally noticeable by the person experiencing because they are repetitive behavior patterns. Such a person can usually break the pattern fairly easily, by themselves or with help. 

The negative part of their conscience ([[Self-Love]]: [[Self-Love#1.1 Conscience|Conscience]]) is not kept in check. Those in a negative loop suppress their emotions instead of expressing them, they have an unfair negative thought, and they don't take corrective actions to break the cycle.

%% [[tmp archive]] %%
>[!info|captain-class archive]+ <span>#10</span> Concept ^loop
>**Negative Loop:** a behavior that describes someone who experiences a cyclic hyperfocus on their emotional pain, external circumstances, or a perceived victimhood, while remaining blind to or excusing their own negative personality traits %% [[Why?]] %% 
> %% [[]] %% %% [[Concepts]] %%

**Cycle**
1. Trigger/Thought: "I can't do this presentation because I won't have anything to say."
2. Behavior: You avoid the presentation to provide short-term relief.
3. Consequence: In the long-term, you are reinforcing your fear and lower self-esteem.
4. Loop: When another presentation comes, your anxiety is much higher because you think you can't do it. 

**Examples** 
*Imposter Syndrome*
1. Thought: "I am not good enough."
2. Behavior: You don't express your emotions. You do more work to compensate.
3. Consequence: You feel empty, and you experience burnout. 
4. Loop: Your performance momentarily drops, which reinforces the belief you are an imposter.

*Depressive Ruination*
1. Trigger: You make a minor mistake.
2. Behavior: You develop a bad mood. You dwell on it constantly, thinking "I always mess everything up."
3. Consequence: Feeling worse makes you less likely to take productive action. You ruminate more.
4. Loop: Your inattention to productive action forces another mistake, which reinforces the belief you mess everything up.

What about you? Let's do an exercise where we explore some of your negative loops. 
## 2.2 Downward Spiral
People experiencing a downward spiral may not recognize it because it becomes an unconscious habit. They don't realize what they are doing is harmful.

%% [[tmp archive]] %%
>[!info|captain-class archive]+ <span>#11</span> Concept ^downward
>**Downward Spiral:** a habitual series of negative loops with momentum, which means they not only repeat, but also get worse %% [[Why?]] %% 
> %% [[]] %% %% [[Concepts]] %%

A downward spiral is like a whirlpool that sucks you close to the center. As the center approaches, it gets more difficult to escape. It's emotionally tiring because we get so caught up that we might be blind to what's happening to us. Sometimes, a person doesn't know they're in a spiral. By the time they see it, their emotional state is so eroded that their ability to fight back is compromised by emotional exhaustion and despair. This is where support groups really save the day.

**Cycle**
1. Trigger/Thought: "I can't make new friends or keep the ones I have."
2. Intense Emotion: You feel angry or even rage at yourself.
3. Ineffective Coping: You snap at your friends, blame them, and push them away from you.
4. Worsening Consequences: You don't notice your behavior is getting worse. The friend abandons you.
5. Loop: You lose another friend, worsening your behavior, reinforcing your belief that you can't maintain or make new friendships.

**How spirals make negativity worse**
1. Crumbling Coping Resources: Physical and mental exhaustion
2. Real Consequences: Inner negativity spills into real-world situations
3. From Pity Party to Despair: External help gone; feeling alone and abandoned
4. Efficient Negative Conscience: Brain becomes efficient at feeling misery; positive thoughts feel unnatural

See how to break the cycle of the downward spiral here.
## 2.3 Misery
A lot of people are not in the best spot in life, and they don't see a way out. Misery is when we feel absolutely trapped by negativity. 

%% [[tmp quote]] %%
>[!info|captain-class quote]+ <span>#8</span> Quote ^misery
> Misery loves company.
> 
>  — Proverb
> %% [[]] %% %% [[Quotes]] %%

 The above quote is very popular, and it could be interpreted three ways.
1. People who are already miserable want happy people to be miserable, so they purposely try to ruin their mood.
2. Miserable people want to find other miserable people so that they don't feel alone.
3. A person who is miserable needs guidance from someone who isn't miserable.

It's difficult to understand why a person is stuck, but there are some common patterns they may fit into. 

**Categories**
1. Preference for misery


%% [[tmp quote]] %%
>[!info|captain-class quote]+ <span>#9</span> Quote ^devil
> The devil you know is better than the devil you don't.
> 
>  — Proverb
> %% [[]] %% %% [[Quotes]] %%

Misery becomes preferable to many people stuck in negativity because they don't know how to be happy. Escaping misery is scary to them because that requires behaving differently, which is more effort. This avoidance to escape can also be attributed to fear of the unknown because chaos and misery is all they've ever known.

2. Addiction to stress and relief

People can feel an addiction to high-intensity situations. People can feel pleasing sensations from stressful conflicts that cause real emotions, like anger and anxiety. Stress hormones, like adrenaline and cortisol, get released into their bodies. They get an additional satisfaction when the circumstances subside and stress-relief hormones, like dopamine or oxytocin, course through their body.

The high-and-low rollercoaster of emotions can feel generally exciting, and that's how they enjoy life, whether the are aware or unware of it.

3. Attention/validation seeking

People want to be seen, and they want their feelings validated. Having a near-constant "pity party" ensures that they get attention, affection, and sympathy. They may fake injuries, illnesses, or other such maladies to gain attention. 

**Pity Parties**
A pity party is where one person experiencing self-pity seeks attention/validation from others. Two or more such people complain to each other about how their lives are miserable. A common theme in such parties is that "no one understands me (us)". 

They may start to believe that happy people are faking their emotions or that they're boring. They believe that the closeness they share is based on real circumstances, but that could be an illusion because the thing that connects them most is trauma. If the co-misery is removed, they may discover that they're actually not compatible. Their commiseration was a ruse, a relationship of convenience. 

4. Thought distortions

Sometimes we are too involved with the details to see the bigger picture ([[Perspective]], [[Analogies]] #2). 

Some people may not be aware with the cognitive dissonance ([[Concepts]] #5) that comes with trying to notice they are in a negative loop. It's a natural thought to believe that they aren't the problem even when they have been shown proof, but everything and everyone else is.

Once a person believes something, they will see reality through the lens of confirming their belief, even if the belief is proven to be false. Their emotional filter only allows negativity, while positivity is filtered out.

Since they only see negativity, they respond with negativity. They use toxic behaviors, like passive-aggression, sarcasm, and ironic comments, to defend themselves against the negativity, never understanding that they are the ones who generate the negativity.

**Examples**
1. If someone believes "nothing good ever happens to me," they may ignore positive opportunities, destroy good relationships, and develop a hyperfocus on negativity.
2. If someone believes "happiness flees from me," they may accept that they will never be happy, learn to love self-pity, and fail to recognize the happiness they already have. 
### 2.3.1 Abusers
There are people in the world who like to hurt people. Whether they were hurt or they simply enjoy negativity, they are people to watch out for. 

**Examples**
1. Machiavellianism (Personal gain)
2. Narcissism (Entitlement; lack of empathy)
3. Psychopathy (Impulsive action; lack of conscience)
4. Sadism (Pleasure from harm)

These can occur individually or all together in what psychologists call the "dark tetrad." They rarely get caught in spirals because they don't accept responsibility for their actions to protect themselves from harm. Even if they lose everyone around them, they discard them easily, claiming they are defective people. They find another group of people and repeat the same patterns.

### 2.3.2 Archetypes of Misery
- Trauma bonds: when abusers and their victims form a toxic bond where the abuser escapes misery by taking it out on the victim; where the victim feels they deserve the treatment and refuse to abandon their abuser because they don't want to hurt them further
- Chronic Complainers: people who have a profound sense of helplessness, crave validation, and who use sarcasm as a shield against their ego; they want you to agree that the world is terrible, but they aren't looking for solutions
	- "Oh, great, another brilliant idea from management. Can't wait to see this fail."
	- "Yeah, but..."
- Energy vampires: people who drain others with their negativity
	- "This vacation is so stupid."
- Commiseration: when miserable people get together to enjoy their misery instead of escape it
	- "No one understands us."
- Defeatists: people who embody learned helplessness and announce the failure of endeavors before they begin
	- "I know that I will mess it up."
- Cynics: people who never exit the "fight or flight mode" and interpret kind or neutral actions as suspicious, manipulative, or stupid
	- "Why are you trying to be nice? What do you want?"
- Deflators: people who use subtle, passive-aggressive remarks to deflate other people's joy
	- "Your new car doesn't have a high safety rating, you know."
- Passive-aggressive: when people use sarcasm, backhanded compliments, the silent treatment, or purposeful delays to inflict emotional damage and gain control, but back down when confronted
	- "It was just a joke; you're too sensitive."
- Virtue Signalers: people who use moral character as a weapon, not to cause change, but to obtain a sense of personable superiority
	- "I stayed up until 3 AM redoing our entire presentation because I didn't want to attach my name to something that low in quality."
- Main Characters: people who see themselves as the center of attention or the most important person in the room and have a severe lack of empathy toward others



# 3. How do I escape misery?
Working through misery alone is hard, and it might even be impossible because of the emotional, mental, and physical drain misery causes. You aren't alone, and there is help available.

The best approach to this is to seek professional counseling resources. They may want to ask about Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT). 

I am not a licensed psychologist, but this is what I have put together through a quick dive and my own experience.

## 3.1 Cognitive Restructuring Guidelines
1. Phase 1: Deconstruction
	1. Be educated to remove blindness
	2. Objectively notice the issue when it happens
	3. Acknowledge and feel the need to change
2. Phase 2: Stabilization
	1. Break isolation by finding support
	2. Assemble a support group of trusted people or professionals
3. Phase 3: Restructuring
	1. Catch the negativity as it happens
		1. Use Body-First Reset: cold water on the face, deep breathing to reset the body and take the mind out of the downward spiral
	2. Use logic instead of rationalization to expand the thought and ask for objective evidence
	3. Integrate what you learn by using behavioral activation: do one positive thing after step 2 to create the dopamine response

## 3.2 Thoughts on Trial
The Courtroom Metaphor is a professionally adapted guide to lead a person through Phase 3 Step 2 of the Cognitive Restructuring Guidelines above. This metaphor is a CBT technique developed by Dr. Aaron T. Beck.

The purpose behind this exercise is to accentuate the process behind the conscience's battle between its positive and negative side. In the client, their negative side usually overpowers the positive side, which explains why they are in a loop.

### 3.2.1 How it works
1. Use a courtroom trial as a metaphor
The metaphor centers around the idea of a courtroom trial for the client's negative thoughts, and the client is asked to play three roles:
- Defense attorney: Gathers objective evidence for the thought
- Prosecutor: Gathers objective evidence against the thought
- Judge: Weighs the evidence and writes a balanced, factual verdict
2. Collaborative Empiricism
The client and the therapist (or support group member) act together as co-investigators who treat the negative thought as an unproven hypothesis rather than reality. 

3. Socratic Method
Rather than arguing with the client, the therapist or member asks structured questions that assist the client in seeing logical flaws in their thinking. 

4. The Thought Record Worksheet
This is the official written record of the process. It contains the following:
- The Claim: ex. "I'm a total failure."
- Exhibit A: Factual data supporting the claim, provided by the defense attorney
- Exhibit B: Factual data disproving the claim, provided by the prosecutor
- Verdict: The new, rational alternative thought

**NOTE ON PEERS ACTING AS THE THERAPIST ROLE**
GUIDE, DON'T DEBATE
1. Don't freestyle the conversation. Place the Thought Record worksheet between them to depersonalize the process and treat the thought as an external object they're examining together.
2. Stick strictly to Socratic Method. Don't judge, don't criticize.
	- What is the hard data supporting that?
	- If I said that about myself, what would you tell me?
	- Are we looking at facts right now, or feelings?
3. Be aware of and avoid risks. Pushing too hard for evidence for a thought that is tied to deep trauma can trigger a severe emotional flashback or dissociation. A therapist knows how to recognize heart rates, breathing patterns, and body language to pull a client back to safety, but a peer doesn't.
4. The best thing for a peer to do is act like a "court clerk" by recording the information on the Thought Record worksheet.

### 3.2.2 Process

1. Isolate the Primary Thought
The thought on trial needs to be identified.
**Strategy**: "If you had to boil all this down into one single sentence, what is the exact thought that runs through your mind?"
*Example:* "I messed up the presentation, so I'm a complete failure and I'm going to get fired."

2. Identify the Cognitive Distortions
Identify logical fallacies merged into that thought as the "charges" brought against it.
**Strategy:** List common cognitive distortions and ask the client to choose theirs.
*Example:* Therapist helps client to see they are engaging in catastrophizing and all-or-nothing thinking.

3. Put the Thought on Trial
The defense attorney and prosecutor collect evidence
**Strategy FOR:** "What is the objective evidence that supports your thought?"
*Example FOR:* "My boss didn't smile, and I stumbled over slide 4."
**Strategy AGAINST:** "Looking over your past, what is the objective evidence that disproves your thought?"
*Example AGAINST:* "I got a great performance review last month. My team asks me for help."

4. Depersonify and Decatastrophize 
Ask questions that change the frame of reference for the client
**Strategy:** 
The Friend Test: "If your best friend came to you with this exact problem, what would you tell them?"
The Time Machine: "Let's look at the worse scenario. What would happen if you did get fired? Is it truly the end of your life, or is it a highly stressful hurdle you have the skills to navigate?"

5. Formulate a "Balanced Thought"
The therapist asks the client to rewrite the original statement by combining the evidence, forming the Bridge Statement that rewires the brain.
**Strategy:** Based on the evidence we just looked at, how can we rewrite your original though into something that is 100% factual?"
*Example:* "I stumbled during a few minutes of my presentation, which feels embarrassing. However, one flawed presentation does not wipe out a year of good performance. My job isn't in immediate danger."
